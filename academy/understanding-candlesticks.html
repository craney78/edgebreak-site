What is a candlestick?

A candlestick represents the price movement of a stock over a specific period of time. Depending on the chart you are viewing, each candle may represent one minute, one hour, one day or even one week of trading activity.

Every candlestick contains four important pieces of information:

  • Opening price
  • Highest price
  • Lowest price
  • Closing price

Together, those values provide a compact visual summary of how price moved during that period.

OpenWhere the period beganThe first traded price represented by that candle.
CloseWhere the period finishedThe final traded price represented by that candle.
HighThe highest price reachedShown by the top of the upper wick.
LowThe lowest price reachedShown by the bottom of the lower wick.

Bullish candles

A bullish candlestick closes above its opening price. This means price finished the period higher than where it began.

Large bullish candles can reflect stronger buying pressure during that period, particularly when they appear near an important level or as part of a broader move.

One bullish candle alone does not confirm a trend or future direction. It should always be read in the context of the surrounding price structure.

Bearish candles

A bearish candlestick closes below its opening price, meaning price finished the period lower than where it began.

Bearish candles are normal even inside healthy uptrends. Markets rarely move in a straight line, and pullbacks or periods of consolidation can form as price develops.

The important question is not whether one candle is red or green, but how that candle fits into the broader market structure.

Understanding wicks

The thin lines extending above and below the candle body are called wicks, sometimes referred to as shadows.

Wicks show where price travelled before moving back toward the closing price. A long upper wick can suggest that higher prices were rejected during the period, while a long lower wick can suggest that lower prices were rejected.

Neither automatically predicts what happens next. Their value comes from the location in which they form and the wider structure around them.

A candlestick is most useful when you ask where it formed, what came before it and how it fits into the broader structure.

Why closing prices matter

Many investors pay close attention to where a candle closes, not simply how far price moved during the session.

A close near the upper end of the candle's range can indicate that buyers maintained control into the end of the period. A close near the lower end can indicate that sellers remained stronger into the close.

During breakout research, closing behaviour around resistance can provide additional context when assessing whether price is holding above, rejecting or repeatedly testing an important level.

Looking beyond individual candles

Beginners often spend too much time searching for individual candlestick patterns. While those formations can provide useful clues, EdgeBreak places greater emphasis on the overall structure of the chart.

One candle rarely tells the complete story. Groups of candlesticks can reveal rising lows, repeated resistance tests, tightening ranges, volatility changes and how price behaves around support and resistance.

Those relationships become much more useful than trying to make a decision from one candle in isolation.

How candlesticks support breakout research

Throughout the Academy, candlesticks become one piece of a much larger research process.

Rather than analysing them in isolation, you will learn to combine candlestick behaviour with:

  • Market structure
  • Support and resistance
  • Higher lows
  • Volume behaviour
  • Unusual market activity
  • EdgeBreak scanner results

When several pieces of evidence support the same interpretation, the chart can become much easier to understand than when relying on a single candlestick pattern.

Lesson summary

Candlesticks provide a visual representation of price movement over time. Understanding candle bodies, wicks and closing prices helps you interpret price action, but their greatest value comes from combining them with wider market structure and additional research evidence.

Key takeaways

  • Every candle shows the open, high, low and close.
  • Bullish candles close above their opening price.
  • Bearish candles close below their opening price.
  • Wicks show the full trading range and possible price rejection.
  • Closing prices provide useful context.
  • Candlesticks should not be analysed in isolation.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Believing one candlestick predicts what happens next.
  • Ignoring the broader market structure.
  • Relying only on named candlestick patterns.
  • Overlooking support, resistance and higher lows.
  • Ignoring volume and wider market context.
